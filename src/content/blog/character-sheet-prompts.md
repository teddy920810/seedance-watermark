The character looks right in the first shot. In the second, her round glasses become rectangular. By the time she turns to open a door, the satchel strap has moved to the other shoulder. These changes are easy to miss while generating individual clips. Once you put the clips together, they can make a single character look like several people.

## Quick Answer

![Start a turnaround with one clear character reference and a short list of features to preserve.](/uploads/blog/character-sheet-prompts/section-1.jpg)

*Start a turnaround with one clear character reference and a short list of features to preserve.*

A character sheet gives you a set of images to check before you start animating. Begin with one approved view of an original character. Use a character sheet prompt to build a matching front, side, and three-quarter view. Add a rear view when the story needs one. Then export clean individual views for the video mode you plan to use. A sheet has done its job when you can choose the right reference quickly and spot a continuity error before it reaches the edit.

## Choose the details that must follow your character

![A front-view concept makes the satchel, glasses, and jacket easy to inspect.](/uploads/blog/character-sheet-prompts/section-2.jpg)

*A front-view concept makes the satchel, glasses, and jacket easy to inspect.*

Write down the few things a viewer would notice if they changed. For an original character called Mira, a bicycle courier, that might be her short curly black hair, oval gold glasses, cropped olive jacket, and canvas satchel. Define left and right from Mira’s perspective. Mark her satchel strap as running from her right shoulder to her left hip. In a straight-on view, the strap starts on the viewer’s left. This distinction matters when you turn her around.

Choose a front image with enough information for the other views. Mira should stand with her feet visible and her arms slightly away from her sides. Her glasses, sleeves, and the bag should have readable outlines. A jacket photographed only from the chest up forces you to invent the hem later. An exaggerated pose makes it difficult to compare limb length across views. Save the expressive pose for the shot where she uses it.

Try this prompt in an image tool that accepts your own design reference. Google’s image prompting guide describes using references as part of an image generation or editing brief. Each image tool handles that input differently. [Google DeepMind’s image prompting guide](https://deepmind.google/models/gemini-image/prompt-guide/)

> Use my approved drawing of Mira as the character reference. Create a full-body front view on a plain pale gray background. She stands upright, her arms hang slightly away from her torso, and her shoes sit on one level floor line. Preserve her oval gold glasses, short black curls, cropped olive jacket, dark cuffed jeans, and tan canvas satchel. The strap runs from Mira’s right shoulder to her left hip. Keep her expression neutral and leave enough room around the figure to crop the image cleanly.

Pick one approved version and save it before generating the next view. Record the original file, the prompt, and any features that only show on one side. For this design, the right sleeve has a sewn patch and the left shoulder has a clean seam. If you call both shoulders “detailed” in a later prompt, the tool can fill the gap with its own interpretation. A two-line checklist gives you something more precise to compare.

## Generate each angle and check the silhouette

![This generated turnaround lines up the figure heights, but the rear-view bag is on the wrong hip for Mira’s brief.](/uploads/blog/character-sheet-prompts/section-3.jpg)

*This generated turnaround lines up the figure heights, but the rear-view bag is on the wrong hip for Mira’s brief.*

A single multi-view contact sheet lets you compare proportions quickly. Ask for evenly spaced figures in the same neutral pose, at the same scale, on a shared floor line. Some tools may crop a shoe, move the arms, or treat each column as permission to redesign the character. If the first pass has these problems, request separate views using the approved front image each time. Build the comparison sheet yourself from the results you select.

**Front view** gives you the baseline for head size, jacket length, and the overall proportions. If Mira’s jacket ends just above the top of the satchel, remember where the two objects meet. In another view, the hem should reach the same part of her body even if the bag covers part of it.

**Side view** gives you the profile, thickness of the glasses, and depth of the bag. Decide which side the shot needs. “View of Mira’s left profile” is easier to check when you say that the satchel rests against the visible hip. If the glasses suddenly look thick enough to obscure her brow, compare against the front view before approving them.

**Three-quarter view** gives you two sides of the face without showing them equally. It is worth preparing if your shot starts with the character looking partly off camera. Ask for a modest turn away from the front. If you need a more exact angle for an edit, use a simple sketch of the intended position as a separate composition reference in a tool with that option.

**Rear view** helps when the character turns away, walks down a hall, or bends over a bike. Check the strap path from the back, where it should stay on the same shoulder, and decide how the hair and jacket collar overlap. A front image cannot settle what a bag buckle looks like from behind. Add the detail to your design notes once you approve it.

> Create Mira’s left-side profile based on the attached approved front view. Keep her standing at the same scale, with the same head size, outfit, and shoe shape. Show the tan canvas bag resting on her visible left hip. Keep her oval glasses, the short curl at the forehead, and the jacket hem at the same height. Use a level camera and a simple gray backdrop.

Read the illustrated turnaround above from left to right: front, three-quarter, profile, and back. The soles line up and the jacket hem stays near the hip. The bag, though, almost disappears in the front view and sits on the viewer’s right in the back view. With Mira facing away, that puts it on her right hip, even though the brief calls for her left. The round glasses are also hard to verify here. Keep the overall proportions as a reference, flag the bag and glasses, and ask for a corrected rear view before turning the sheet into a shot.

## Prepare references for the shots you will animate

![A separate scene frame puts the approved profile and bicycle into position.](/uploads/blog/character-sheet-prompts/section-4.jpg)

*A separate scene frame puts the approved profile and bicycle into position.*

Save the complete sheet for continuity checks and each view as its own file for uploading. Leave the background simple and a little space around the figure. Keep file names descriptive, such as “mira-left-profile” or “mira-front,” so you can find the right view halfway through an edit. If the sheet has labels or corner marks, keep them in the planning copy. A clean upload helps keep the focus on the character.

Match the asset to the type of input the video tool offers. When it accepts a starting frame, upload an image that already looks like the first moment of the shot. For a profile shot of Mira pushing her bicycle, use a composed frame of her beside the bicycle. A profile cutout is useful for preparing that frame. The camera position, ground, and contact between her hand and the handlebar deserve their own check before you animate.

With a dedicated character or subject reference field, use the view that makes the features at risk easiest to see. A three-quarter portrait gives you both the shape of the glasses and the placement of the visible curl. If the interface accepts multiple reference images, try two views of the same costume and age. Read the labels for each input so you know whether it describes a person, a starting frame, a style, or something else. 

For early planning, you can also use the [Seedances.co text-to-video settings preview](/seedance-ai-generated) to outline a scene and choose a target duration. Use a mode that explicitly accepts image input when you are ready to feed it the finished character reference. Keep an approved still from the first usable clip beside your sheet. It can become a better visual target for later footage if the camera, costume, and story timing already match.

## Frequently asked questions

![Profile and three-quarter references show how features read from different angles.](/uploads/blog/character-sheet-prompts/section-5.jpg)

*Profile and three-quarter references show how features read from different angles.*

**Should I generate one sheet or several separate images?** Use one sheet to compare the design. Export separate images for upload and for correcting individual poses. If the tool mixes wardrobe details across columns or crops the hands in a grid, generate views individually from the same approved image. Assemble your own contact sheet after checking each view.

**How do I keep the face consistent when the character turns?** Compare the face in a gentle three-quarter turn before trying a full profile. Give the model a clear approved reference that exposes the identifying features you need. Keep the lighting even for the first attempt so shadows do not obscure the brow or the shape of the nose. In a video test, look at several frames through the turn. The start and end can both look right even when the glasses or jaw change halfway through.

**Can I upload the whole sheet to a video generator?** Check the input field. In a starting-frame slot, the generator can try to animate the entire grid because it sees the grid as a scene. A separate drawing of the relevant angle is easier to position. If the mode accepts character references, follow that mode’s instructions for the number of images and check how it treats different views.

**What should I do when a costume detail keeps swapping sides?** Write which side belongs to the character and which side appears on screen in the intended view. Compare the error against the approved sheet. If a left-side badge keeps moving, prepare a start frame with the badge clearly visible or choose a crop that avoids the swap. Preserve the corrected still as a reference for the next shot.

## Conclusion

![Separate views give an animator a clean reference for each camera angle.](/uploads/blog/character-sheet-prompts/section-6.jpg)

*Separate views give an animator a clean reference for each camera angle.*

Write a small motion prompt for the first check. “Mira turns her head toward the bicycle bell while keeping her left hand on the handlebar” gives you a clear result to inspect over a short clip. Watch the glasses during the turn and check the satchel at the end. Approve the continuity before you increase the action. If the model changes the bag when she turns, narrow the turn or start from a view that already shows the bag. Keep the prompt otherwise steady so you can see whether the narrower turn helps.
