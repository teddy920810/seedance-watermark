You ask for a quiet walk through a rainy street. Seedance gives you three cuts, a face that changes halfway through, and a hand that melts into an umbrella. The prompt sounded cinematic. It was not easy to direct.

AI video prompting works like giving instructions to a film crew with only seconds to shoot. The crew needs a subject, one action, a setting, and a camera plan. Give it five actions and three camera moves, and it starts choosing for you.

This guide gives you a repeatable formula, copy-ready Seedance prompt examples, and practical fixes for the failures that waste the most credits.

## The Seedance prompt formula

Start with seven parts: **shot type + subject + one action + setting + camera movement + lighting and style + constraints**.

Here is a complete example:

*Medium tracking shot of a woman in a yellow raincoat walking through a narrow Tokyo side street at night. She looks into one shop window and smiles. The camera moves beside her at walking speed with a 50mm lens. Wet pavement reflects warm signs. Natural motion, realistic skin, continuous shot, no cuts, no readable text.*

Keep the action simple. If the first result works, add one new idea in the next generation.

## Why detailed prompts still fail

Length is not the same as control. A long prompt can contain instructions that compete with one another. “The camera stays fixed” conflicts with “orbit around the subject.” “A calm product reveal” fights with “rapid montage.” When Seedance cannot satisfy everything, it may invent a cut, ignore an action, or change the subject.

Too many actions create another problem. A person cannot realistically enter a room, pick up a cup, drink, turn to camera, speak, and leave in a short clip. Break that sequence into shots. Generate each shot separately, then assemble them in an editor.

Image-to-video prompts fail for a different reason. The image already defines the person, clothing, composition, and background. Repeating every visible detail spends words on information the model has. Describe what should move, how the camera should move, and what must remain stable.

## Build the prompt one decision at a time

### Choose the shot and subject anchor

Name the framing first: wide shot, medium shot, close-up, macro, overhead, or point of view. Then identify the subject with a short, stable description. “A woman” is weak if you need continuity. “A woman in her thirties with a blunt black bob and a yellow raincoat” gives the model anchors it can repeat.

### Give the subject one visible action

Use an action a camera can record: turns a page, lifts a bottle, walks toward a doorway, or looks over one shoulder. Feelings help only when they change the performance. Replace “she feels hopeful” with “her shoulders relax and she gives a small smile.”

### Define the environment

Mention the location, time, weather, and one or two useful background behaviors. “A quiet diner at dawn, rain moving down the windows” is easier to stage than a paragraph of decorative detail.

### Direct the camera

State one camera behavior. A slow dolly-in adds intimacy. A lateral tracking shot follows movement. A locked camera keeps product geometry steadier. Lens language can help, but it should support the shot rather than decorate it.

### Set light and visual treatment

Name the source and quality of light: soft window light, hard noon sun, warm tungsten practicals, or cool overcast daylight. Add a recognizable treatment such as documentary realism, hand-painted animation, or polished commercial photography.

### Add timing audio and constraints

When the interface supports audio, describe a short sound cue or one line of dialogue. Finish with constraints that protect the shot: continuous take, stable face, unchanged clothing, fixed product label, no extra people, or no readable text.

**Weak:** A cinematic woman walks in the rain, dramatic camera, beautiful lights.

Camera remains at shoulder height and matches her speed. Wet street, warm practical lights, realistic night photography, stable face and clothing, continuous take, no cuts.

## 12 copy-ready Seedance prompt examples

Use these as starting points. Replace details in brackets, then test one change at a time.

1. **Cinematic portrait:** “Close-up of \[subject\] beside a train window. Their eyes follow passing lights. Slow dolly-in, shallow depth of field, soft blue evening light, realistic skin, one continuous shot.”
2. **Product turntable:** “Locked medium close-up of \[product\] on a matte stone pedestal. The pedestal rotates one quarter turn clockwise. Soft top light and narrow rim light, accurate shape and color, label unchanged.”
3. **Anime motion:** “Wide shot of \[character\] standing on a rooftop as wind lifts the coat hem. Slow push-in, hand-drawn anime, clean line work, stable face and costume.”
4. **Social hook:** “Vertical close-up of a sealed \[object\]. It opens to reveal \[surprise\]. Fast push-in, bright studio light, centered subject, empty lower third for captions.”
5. **Dialogue:** “Medium two-shot of \[speaker A\] on the left and \[speaker B\] on the right. A says, ‘\[short line\].’ B listens. Fixed camera, natural room tone, stable positions.”
6. **Landscape:** “Wide aerial view of \[landscape\] at sunrise. Low fog moves through the valley as the camera glides forward. Natural color, no buildings or people.”
7. **Transformation:** “Locked full-body shot of \[subject\]. Frost grows from shoes to shoulders over five seconds. Background and silhouette remain fixed, continuous transformation, no cut.”
8. **Tracking shot:** “Rear three-quarter tracking shot of \[subject\] riding a bicycle along \[location\]. Camera follows at constant distance. Realistic wheel motion, stable proportions, late-afternoon light.”
9. **Fixed camera:** “Static wide shot of a kitchen. \[Subject\] enters from frame right, places one bag on the counter, and stops. Natural daylight, no camera movement or cut.”
10. **Macro:** “Macro shot of condensation on a cold \[container\]. One droplet slides down. Camera locked, crisp texture, dark background, rim light, label geometry unchanged.”
11. **Multi-shot story:** “Wide view of \[character\] at an empty bus stop. Close-up as headlights reflect in their eyes. The bus passes without stopping. Same character, coat, rain, and palette.” Use only with multi-shot support.
12. **Image to video:** “Preserve the person, clothing, background, and framing. The person turns toward camera and blinks once. Curtains move slightly. Gentle handheld drift, no cut, no new objects.”

## Camera movements Seedance can follow

Simple camera moves usually produce cleaner results. A **dolly-in** moves the camera toward the subject. A **pan** rotates left or right from one position. A **tilt** rotates up or down. A **tracking shot** moves with the subject. A **crane move** changes height. **Handheld** adds small imperfections. A **locked shot** does not move.

An orbit asks the model to reveal changing sides of a subject and background at the same time. It can work, but a full orbit around a moving person is demanding. Begin with a short quarter orbit around a stationary subject. If geometry bends, switch to a slow lateral move or use a reference image.

## Keep characters and scenes consistent

Write a reusable identity block and paste it into every related shot. Keep age range, face shape, hair, signature clothing, and one prop unchanged. Do the same for the location: layout, time of day, palette, and main light source.

Reference images can provide stronger anchors when the model and interface expose that feature. Use clean front, three-quarter, and profile views. Avoid references that disagree about hair, wardrobe, or age. Generate an approved starting frame before asking for complex movement.

Change one variable per iteration. If you change wardrobe, camera, weather, and action together, you will not know which change caused the face to drift.

## Fix common Seedance prompt failures

| Failure | Remove | Add or change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Random cuts | Multiple locations and actions | “Continuous shot, no cuts” and one action |
| Warped motion | Complex spins or object contact | Slower motion, simpler path, shorter clip |
| Face drift | Vague subject wording | Stable identity block or reference image |
| Weak motion | Abstract verbs such as “feels” | Visible movement with direction and timing |
| Ignored style | Several competing styles | One medium, palette, and lighting treatment |

If the composition is right but identity keeps changing, switch from text-to-video to image-to-video. If hands break during contact, separate the action into a before shot, a brief contact shot, and an after shot.

## A faster iteration workflow

Test the hardest shot first. Use a lower-cost or faster mode when the interface offers one. First lock composition, then motion, then finish details such as light and texture. Save the prompt and settings for every keeper. Render higher quality only after the action works. This turns credits into evidence instead of a pile of nearly identical failures.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a Seedance prompt be

Use enough detail to remove ambiguity, often 40 to 100 words for one shot. A shorter direct prompt beats a long prompt with conflicting actions.

### Does Seedance support negative prompts

Support depends on the model and access platform. If there is no separate negative field, place a few concrete constraints at the end of the main prompt. Verify the current interface before relying on the feature.

### How do I stop Seedance from changing faces

Repeat a compact identity block, use consistent references where available, simplify the action, and change one variable at a time. Image-to-video usually provides a stronger starting anchor.

### Should I use text to video or image to video

Use text-to-video for exploration. Use image-to-video when composition, product geometry, or character identity matters more than surprise.

### How do I prompt a continuous shot with no cuts

Ask for one action and one camera move, then add “single continuous take, no cuts.” Remove words such as montage, sequence, and multiple angles.
