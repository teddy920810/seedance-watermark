You finally get a beautiful shot. Your character opens the garden gate, sunlight catches the flowers, and the clip ends before anything else happens. The story in your head lasts a minute. The file on your computer lasts a few seconds.

That gap does not mean you chose the wrong tool. It means you need to distinguish a generated shot from an edited video. Think of a clip as one building block. A longer finished video uses several blocks, with decisions about the joins.

## The short answer

How long can an AI generated video be? There is no single industry limit. One generation follows the selected model and interface settings. Extensions add another segment where supported. An edited film combines separate shots and can run much longer, subject to the editor and destination platform. A tool advertising a long finished video may be assembling scenes rather than generating one uninterrupted take.

For a beginner, a useful first goal is a clear thirty to sixty second story built from short shots. That is a production recommendation, not a universal technical ceiling. Choose the length because the idea needs it, not because the export menu allows it.

## Three lengths that mean different things

**Single generation length** is the duration requested for one output. Check the exact model, mode, input type, resolution, and selected platform. A model name alone is not a complete specification.

**Extended length** includes a continuation. The system uses an earlier clip or its ending to produce more material. A continuation can introduce a new expression, an altered object, or different lighting. More seconds do not automatically mean an acceptable continuous shot.

**Finished video length** is the duration after editing. It may include generated footage, real recordings, still images, narration, titles, and pauses. Those ingredients can tell one coherent story without pretending every frame came from a single generation.

This distinction answers the practical question behind many length searches: you usually need a plan for a longer video, rather than a longer setting for every shot.

## Read the model limit in its proper context

As checked on November 22, 2026, [Runway lists Gen 4.5 durations from two to ten seconds](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/46974685288467-Creating-with-Gen-4-5). Its [older Gen 4 documentation lists five and ten second outputs](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/37327109429011-Creating-with-Gen-4-Video). These are model specific examples, not the minimum and maximum of all AI video tools.

For another current example, [Google's November announcement describes native fifteen second Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash videos](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/introducing-gemini-omni-1-2-flash/). That announcement does not establish which duration every personal account or third party interface exposes.

| What you see | What it establishes | What still needs checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A duration selector | Available choices for that interface | Model, mode, and account conditions |
| An Extend control | A supported continuation route | Source eligibility, added length, and output settings |
| A long video promise | A claimed finished runtime | Whether scenes, stock, or stills are assembled |
| A five second default | The initial selection | The maximum and the actual export workflow |

Before buying access for a particular project, open the relevant documentation and the actual settings screen. Confirm whether a quoted number refers to one shot, one continuation, or the entire project. Save those details with your shot notes so an update does not make your earlier results confusing.

## Why the longest available shot may be the wrong choice

A shot needs enough time for its action. A small head turn and a person crossing a room are different timing problems. Asking both to fit the same short duration can make one feel rushed and the other feel empty.

Longer shots also create more material to inspect. A face can look convincing at the opening and change near the ending. A backpack strap may slip through a shoulder. A label can become unreadable halfway through a camera move.

Watch the clip while it moves, then scrub the beginning, middle, and end. Decide how much is usable. A ten second output with three reliable seconds is not ten seconds of finished footage. Keep that distinction in your budget as well as your edit.

## Choose between extending and making a new shot

Extend when the viewer needs to remain in the same moment: a subject finishing a turn, a slow reveal reaching its destination, or a quiet environment continuing naturally. First check whether your chosen tool supports that source and extension route.

Make a new shot when the story moves to a new viewpoint or action. A close up of a hand placing a seed can cut to a wider view of a plant. You do not need to preserve one continuous camera move to make those moments feel connected.

Neither route guarantees identity. Compare the continuation against the original reference, not only the previous generated frame. Otherwise a small change can become the new starting point and drift further in each segment.

## Plan a longer video without losing continuity

Start with one sentence about the finished story. For example: a camper arrives at a lake, sets down a pack, and settles in for sunset. That is enough for a simple sequence. It does not require a complicated plot or a dozen locations.

Write the ending before requesting any footage. If the last image should be the orange tent reflected in still water, protect that destination. Every earlier shot should help the viewer understand how the camper reached it.

### Turn the idea into a shot list

Separate location, action, and detail. Use a wide view to establish the lake, a medium view for the pack being set down, and a close view for a small meaningful action. Give each shot one job.

Choose an approximate edited duration for each beat. Then request enough source material to allow trimming. The generated duration and the final screen time do not have to match. Cutting away before an action finishes can feel cleaner than holding on an unreliable ending.

Add a small amount of connecting material. Water moving, a boot stepping onto the path, or a tent detail can provide a useful cutaway when two character shots do not join convincingly. Generate it for a storytelling reason rather than as random filler.

### Make a continuity note before every generation

Record the character description, clothing, important props, time of day, light direction, and camera viewpoint. Keep the approved reference images together. A short list is easier to check than a large paragraph full of decorative adjectives.

For our camper, the orange tent, dark backpack, and warm late afternoon light are the anchors. If the next shot changes all three, the audience has to work out whether it is still the same trip.

### Use the last frame only when it helps

For a continued action, an acceptable final frame can be a useful starting image. [Runway documents this last frame workflow](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/48324313115155-Image-to-Video-Prompting-Guide), followed by combining the outputs in an editor. Inspect that frame first. A distorted hand is a poor foundation for the next shot.

If the ending is already damaged, select an earlier clean frame or return to the approved reference. Do not preserve a bad frame simply because it happens to be last.

### Check the join rather than hiding it

Place both clips on the timeline. Watch the cut at normal speed. Check the subject position, direction of travel, color, and action timing. Remove duplicated frames or an awkward pause when necessary.

A transition effect can soften an intentional scene change. It cannot reliably disguise a face changing identity. If the join is distracting, try a cutaway, a different angle, or a replacement shot before adding more effects.

## Budget for usable seconds and retries

Use a simple planning example. Suppose a sixty second piece needs twelve shots averaging five edited seconds each. If you request two takes per shot, that is twenty four generations before replacements. This is illustrative arithmetic, not a promised success rate.

Track the billed work, the accepted shots, and the seconds you actually use. An inexpensive attempt can become costly if you repeatedly discard it. Conversely, a more expensive model can still be a poor choice if its extra detail does not solve your specific continuity problem.

Set a retry limit before starting. When a shot repeatedly fails, simplify the action, change the view, or film that beat yourself. Continuing the same request indefinitely is not a production strategy.

## FAQ

### Can AI create a one minute video

Yes, a one minute finished video can be assembled from separate shots. Whether one continuous generation or extension can reach that length depends on the exact model and platform. Check those routes separately.

### How do I join clips without changing the face

Use approved references and compare every shot against them. Keep appearance and lighting consistent. When a direct continuation fails, change the angle or insert a relevant cutaway. There is no wording that guarantees perfect identity.

### Why do different models have different duration limits

They expose different generation modes, controls, and resource limits. Hosting platforms can also offer different settings for the same model family. Read the current version specific documentation rather than applying another tool's number.

## Final recommendation

Choose the finished story length first, then choose a production route. Extend for a genuine continuation. Cut to a new shot when the idea changes. If continuity repeatedly breaks, simplify or replace the difficult beat. A shorter, understandable sequence gives you more useful practice than a longer clip you cannot confidently publish.
