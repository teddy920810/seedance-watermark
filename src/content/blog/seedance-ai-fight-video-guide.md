For creators, the practical lesson is not “paste two celebrity names and publish.” It is to separate a compelling action idea from the people, characters, reference assets and permissions involved. You can study scene geography and timing while developing an original fictional rehearsal instead of copying an actor's identity or a franchise costume.

## Separate the people, characters and permissions

Real-person likeness and fictional-character rights are different review problems. Replacing a celebrity with Spider-Man does not automatically remove the need for permission checks; replacing Spider-Man with a newly invented character does not license a copied soundtrack or reference clip.

| Layer | Question to resolve before production | What does not resolve it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Person and voice | Do you have appropriate permission for the intended synthetic depiction and distribution? | A public photograph, recognizable face or downloadable interview |
| Character and design | Are the character, costume and distinctive assets original or appropriately licensed? | Calling a recognizable franchise recreation a fan experiment |
| Reference material | Can you use the image, footage, choreography recording and music for this purpose? | Finding it in a search result |
| Provider | Does this service allow this content, input and use under its current rules? | Another provider accepting a similar prompt |
| Distribution | Are disclosure, privacy and other destination-platform requirements satisfied? | Adding a disclaimer only to your project notes |

On February 13, 2026, [SAG-AFTRA objected](<https://www.sagaftra.org/sag-aftra-statement-seedance-20>) to what it characterized as unauthorized uses of performers' voices and likenesses enabled by Seedance. That is an important industry position, not a court judgment about every output or proof of the permissions attached to one particular file.

The U.S. Copyright Office's [2024 digital-replica announcement](<https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2024/1048.html?loclr=eanco>) explains why realistic false depictions raise a policy issue of their own. Its recommendation is historical context, not a statement of every law in force today. A commercial project involving recognizable people should receive current, jurisdiction-specific review.

For copyright, the Office's [fair-use guidance](<https://copyright.gov/fair-use/>) weighs purpose, the original work's nature, the amount used and market effects. No universal number of seconds guarantees fair use. A noncommercial label or parody claim does not settle the analysis on its own. This article provides production-risk orientation, not legal advice or clearance for a specific video.

## A practical original-character rehearsal workflow

The following exercise studies readable action without requiring a famous identity. Its intended deliverable is a five-second, non-contact stage rehearsal between two original adult characters. It is a proposed workflow, not a report that a model successfully rendered the scene.

### Define the movement before the appearance

Write one action beat: a performer feints, the second steps back, and both stop. Keep one continuous wide composition so that positions remain understandable. Give the characters simple, different clothing colors rather than elaborate armor or recognizable emblems. Their objective is to demonstrate controlled spacing, not injury or a convincing celebrity impersonation.

Create a small reference packet only if you own or have appropriate rights to its contents. Use original character sheets, an empty studio image and a movement sketch, for example. If filming a reference rehearsal, work with qualified performers and appropriate safety practices; do not attempt dangerous stunts merely to supply an AI input.

### Use a bounded, original prompt

Here is an original brief you can adapt to a provider's supported duration and content rules:

\> Original fictional rehearsal, five-second continuous wide shot: two adult stage performers in plain teal and ochre training clothes in a bright rehearsal studio. One slow feint, a clear step back, then both pause at arm's length. The action is choreographed and non-contact; no injury, blood, weapons, famous faces, logos or dialogue. Keep performer positions and clothing consistent.

If the service rejects a request, inspect its rules and revise the legitimate content or seek clarification. Do not disguise identities, obscure a face to evade a restriction or move prohibited material through a different input channel. Provider acceptance would still not supply rights to third-party assets.

### Review motion, identity and editability separately

After an authorized generation, review the entire clip at normal speed and at key action moments. Track whether the teal performer stays on the intended side, whether hands remain plausible, whether feet slide and whether the retreat actually creates space. Reject unintended contact, sudden identity changes, costume swaps or an unrequested camera cut.

Then review the export as an editor. Is there a stable beginning and ending? Does the shot leave room for a title added later? Is the audio appropriate, or should you replace it with an original or properly licensed track? Avoid using a celebrity voice as a shortcut for atmosphere. As one provider-specific example, [Runway requires explicit consent and verification for custom voice training](<https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/29828787723411-Voice-verification>); that rule is not a universal description of every video model.

For another attempt, change one variable at a time—simpler motion, clearer spacing or a wider view—and retain the earlier settings. That makes improvement attributable. Record all paid attempts and editing time when calculating cost per accepted shot, rather than comparing only the price of the final successful generation.

## Disclosure before distribution

Before uploading, review the destination's current rules rather than relying on a caption copied from an old tutorial. [YouTube's current help](<https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en>) requires disclosure for realistic AI-generated or meaningfully altered content, including depictions that make a person appear to do something they did not do or show a realistic event that never happened. Its documented upload control is now “AI use” under “Attributes”; select the appropriate answer for your content.

A plain-language description such as “AI-generated fictional rehearsal featuring original characters” can add context, but it does not replace the required platform setting. Avoid packaging the video as leaked footage, a genuine confrontation or a performer endorsement.

Disclosure is also not immunity from other requirements. YouTube's [privacy guidance](<https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801895?hl=en>) allows people to request review of realistic synthetic depictions of their likeness; disclosure, identifiability and context are among the considerations. Keep a project record containing permissions, reference provenance, prompts, settings, accepted takes and final captions so you can explain what you made and how it was presented.

## FAQ

### Did Brad Pitt and Tom Cruise actually film the Seedance fight?

The public material reviewed here does not establish their participation. Describe it as a synthetic depiction attributed to Seedance, not footage of a real confrontation. “Seedance Brad Pitt vs Tom Cruise” and the reversed name order refer to the same search topic, not evidence of separate filmed contests.

### Does “deepfake video” prove it was made with face swapping?

No. People use deepfake as a broad description of convincing synthetic likenesses. A repost title does not identify the complete technical process. Without underlying assets and project history, do not claim to know whether face replacement, reference video or other editing was involved.

### Can I make Spider-Man versus Captain America or Venom?

The queries “Spider-Man vs Captain America Seedance 2.0” and “Spider-Man vs Venom Seedance 2.0” raise character and asset questions, not just face-consent questions. Check the intended use and provider rules, and obtain appropriate review or permissions. For an ordinary practice exercise, original characters avoid the need to reproduce those particular franchise identities.

### Is a short, noncommercial fan fight automatically safe to post?

No automatic clearance follows from length, lack of revenue or an “AI” label. Review character assets, likenesses, source materials and destination rules separately. A disclaimer helps communicate context but cannot supply permission or determine a disputed legal exception.

### How can an ordinary user compare action-video attempts fairly?

Use the same original characters, movement beat, framing and target length. Log settings, every paid attempt and editing effort. Judge spacing, anatomy, identity continuity and usable starts and endings before discussing a winner. This guide offers that review method; it does not claim to have run a multi-provider benchmark.
