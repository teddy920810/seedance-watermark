You ask for a gardener by a potting bench. The result has lovely light, an elaborate greenhouse, and a person too small to recognize. You add “more realistic.” Now the light changes, but the gardener is still lost in the background.

The missing instruction was about framing. Better adjectives could not reliably solve it. A useful image prompt works like a short set of decisions: what must be visible, where it belongs, and which details matter enough to preserve.

This guide focuses on still images. Movement, shot duration, and camera choreography belong in a later video prompt, after you approve the picture.

## Quick Answer

Good AI prompts for image generation describe a visible subject, a clear composition, a setting, and suitable light. Start with the most important requirement and use ordinary language. Set the aspect ratio in the interface where available. Inspect the result against a short checklist, then change one instruction at a time. Use a reference image when exact identity or object appearance matters.

## Decide what the image must accomplish

Before writing, complete this sentence: “This image needs to show…” A close portrait, an inviting room, and a recognizable product need different instructions. They should not begin with the same long list of fashionable style terms.

For a personal gardening post, you might need a friendly person beside a bench with empty space for a caption. For an animation reference, you might need their hands fully visible and their body separated from surrounding tools. Both pictures can be attractive. Only one may suit your next task.

Write three acceptance conditions. For our gardener, use a waist up view, a plain apron, and one small watering can. These conditions give you something concrete to review. “Make it beautiful” gives you a preference without telling you how to correct a failure.

## Build a still image prompt you can revise

*Original editorial examples for composition, lighting, and material description. Results will vary across tools and settings.*

**Best for:** making a new portrait, scene, or object image when you can choose the composition. This method is less suitable for preserving an exact person's likeness or reproducing a real product from text alone. Prepare a permitted reference for those tasks.

### Start with the subject and framing

Name who or what belongs in the frame. Then describe how much of it the viewer should see. “Waist up portrait” solves a different problem from “wide view of a garden.” If the face matters, make that clear through framing rather than relying on “highly detailed.”

Describe a few visible traits. Clothing, hairstyle, expression, and posture are useful. A long fictional biography usually does little to tell the generator what should appear. For real people, do not assume a description can establish identity or consent.

Try this original people template:

> Editorial photograph of [adult subject], shown [framing], wearing [simple clothing], with [visible expression or pose]. Place the subject [position] in [setting]. Use [light direction and quality]. Keep [important visible detail] clear.

Our example becomes:

> Editorial photograph of an adult gardener with short silver hair, shown from the waist up in a plain charcoal apron. She stands beside a potting bench, holding a small cobalt watering can at waist height. Soft daylight enters from the left. Keep both hands and the complete watering can visible.

Treat that as a starting specification, not a guaranteed result. Check the grip and the object before saving it as a reference.

### Give the scene a spatial arrangement

A scene prompt should explain where things sit relative to each other. Choose a viewpoint, a main focal point, and a few supporting elements. Too many equally important objects can make a room or landscape difficult to read.

This is particularly useful when your output has a practical purpose. If you need room for a headline, specify an uncluttered area. If you plan a later camera move, leave appropriate space around the subject. Neither requirement is automatically satisfied by asking for a cinematic scene.

Use this scene template:

> [Medium] of [location] viewed from [viewpoint]. Place [main element] in [position], with [supporting elements] nearby. Use [time and light]. Keep [area] simple for [intended purpose].

For example:

> Natural editorial photograph of a small backyard potting area viewed at bench height. Place a weathered wooden bench on the right, with two terracotta pots and a closed blue watering can nearby. Use soft morning light. Keep the left third uncluttered for a short caption added later.

The final phrase states a layout requirement. It does not ask the model to invent the caption. Add important text yourself when you need exact wording and reliable placement.

### Describe a prop through shape and material

Props become easier to judge when the prompt names visible properties. A watering can needs a body, handle, spout, and believable proportions. “Premium object” says little about those relationships.

Use a plain background while exploring shape. Busy scenery can hide a defect that becomes obvious during animation. Once the object works, introduce a setting through an edit or a new controlled attempt.

Try this prop template:

> Studio photograph of [object] made from [material], in [color], viewed from [angle]. Show [important parts] clearly. Place it on [surface] against [background], lit by [light]. Avoid [unnecessary decoration].

For example:

> Studio photograph of a small cobalt enamel watering can viewed from a three quarter angle. Show one handle, one spout, and the full base clearly. Place it on pale wood against a neutral background, with soft light from the left. No lettering, flowers, or extra accessories.

For an actual item you own, upload its photograph where the tool supports it. Text alone invites the model to invent a similar object, including details you may not want changed.

### Choose one visual treatment

Decide whether you want a photograph, illustration, painting, or another medium. Then add only the style information that supports the task. “Watercolor illustration” and “natural documentary photograph” describe different outcomes. Combining several contradictory treatments makes evaluation harder.

Lighting should also be concrete. Describe its source, direction, and softness when relevant. [Adobe's prompt guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/firefly/web/work-with-images/generate-images/writing-effective-text-prompts.html) recommends descriptive, direct language rather than relying on elaborate technical wording. You do not need to imitate a camera manual to explain a picture.

### Generate and inspect before adding complexity

Set the canvas or aspect ratio in the generator. Save the exact prompt and model label. Generate a small initial set within your budget, then compare each result against the three conditions you wrote earlier.

Look at the whole composition first. Then inspect hands, faces, object geometry, and text at the intended viewing size. If the picture fails its main purpose, do not spend your next attempt polishing a minor detail.

The method's strength is traceability: you can connect a requested change to a visible result. Its limitation is that generation remains variable. The same instruction can produce different images across models and attempts.

## Fix composition with a specific instruction

If the gardener is too small, try “Frame her from the waist up and remove most of the background.” If the watering can blocks her face, specify where she holds it. If the image is cluttered, remove competing objects instead of adding more detail to the subject.

Avoid rewriting the entire prompt immediately. A large rewrite can change the image in several ways, leaving you unsure which edit helped. [Meta's image prompting guide](https://meta.ai/learn/ai-creativity/prompts-for-ai-images-10-examples-and-tips-for-better-results/) also recommends refining one variable at a time. Treat that as a practical comparison habit rather than a promise of pixel perfect preservation.

When a required layout repeatedly fails, try a simple sketch or a supported composition reference. Some requirements are easier to communicate visually than through another paragraph of text.

## Change one element while checking everything else

Duplicate the accepted image before editing. Name the requested change and the details that should remain. For example: “Change only the watering can from cobalt blue to olive green. Keep its shape, the person's pose, the lighting, and the background.”

After editing, compare more than the color. Check the handle, spout, hand position, face, and shadows. If unrelated details changed, return to the approved source. Use a smaller supported selection or a more constrained edit when the tool provides one.

Do not chain endless revisions onto a deteriorating image. Save meaningful versions. A clear filename and the associated prompt make it easier to return to the last usable state.

## FAQ

### How do I prompt people scenes and props together

State which element is primary. Describe the person's framing, the scene's arrangement, and the prop's position in separate short sentences. Start with a simple interaction. Check the hands and contact points before adding more objects.

### Why does the generator ignore my composition

The prompt may contain competing viewpoints or too many focal points. Simplify it, set the canvas explicitly, and describe the desired framing directly. Use a supported visual reference when words alone do not communicate the arrangement.

### Can I change one detail and preserve everything else

That is an editing goal, not a universal guarantee. Work from a duplicate, use a supported local selection when appropriate, and compare the full result. Return to the original if an edit changes something important.

## Conclusion

Begin with the image's purpose and three visible requirements. Build a prompt around the subject, layout, material, and light that serve those requirements. When the result is almost right, make a small, testable correction. Save the approved still before moving into video. That habit makes your prompts useful beyond a single lucky generation.
